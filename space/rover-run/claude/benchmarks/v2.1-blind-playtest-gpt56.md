# Rover Run blind playtest

## Run-by-run log

### Run 1 — score 3

Ended on row 3 by driving into SAND. I started with a blind “safe” guess, then sampled row 2’s ground and saw `soft`. I wrongly treated soft itself as danger even though that row was safe. The useful discovery came one row later: a soft card followed by a flat map row produced SAND. After the run I kept `soft + flat -> has SAND`.

### Run 2 — score 14

Ended on row 9 in a STORM after clearing eight rows. I correctly reused the soft-to-flat sand rule at row 3 and detoured. I became too conservative on rows 5 and 6, calling danger and detouring around rows that turned out safe. Later, firm+haze followed by steep was safe twice, so I trusted it on row 9; the exact-looking pattern then produced a storm. I changed from treating card+slope pairs as deterministic to treating most of them as odds. I kept `haze + steep -> is safe`, which the notebook rated “sometimes,” 2 of 3 at that point.

### Run 3 — score 8

Ended on row 5 in a STORM after clearing four rows. The first four rows mostly repeated the familiar opening, including the soft-to-flat sand trap. I then trusted firm+haze followed by tilted because it had been safe before, but it produced a storm this time. I stopped assuming that a successful pair in one solution would replay identically in another. I kept `firm + not steep -> is safe`, then supported in 4 of 5 cases.

### Run 4 — score 30

Finished all 14 rows with 13 correct calls and one wrong final call; the finish bonus was +3. This was the run where selective sampling worked:

- A ground sample found SAND on row 4.
- Sampling both channels showed row 5 was safe.
- A dust sample helped clear row 7.
- A dust sample found STORM on row 11.
- The learned soft-to-non-steep rule correctly predicted SAND on row 12.
- A ground sample resolved row 13.
- I detoured around the uncertain final row; it contained STORM, although I had called it safe.

I changed from sampling whenever a pair was unfamiliar to sampling only the unresolved hazard channel. I kept the generalized rule `soft + not steep -> has SAND`; the notebook rated it “always,” 5 of 5.

### Run 5 — score 4

Ended on row 3 in a STORM after clearing two rows. The opening dust card was haze instead of the usual clear. I sampled row 2’s dust and found haze, safely drove it, then assumed the repeated firm+haze followed by flat combination would also make row 3 safe. It instead produced STORM. This was the bluntest evidence that consecutive identical-looking card+slope inputs can have different outcomes. I kept `haze + flat -> has STORM`; the notebook rated it “sometimes,” 1 of 3.

## Rules I believe drive the hidden cards

- **Soft followed by a flat or tilted (“not steep”) row produces SAND — high confidence.** The notebook showed 5 of 5, always. The narrower soft+flat version was 3 of 3. This was the only rule I would trust enough to detour without sampling.
- **Soft followed by steep usually avoids SAND — medium confidence.** I saw several safe-ground outcomes and no remembered sand outcome for this pair, but storms remain independent, so this does not imply the whole row is safe.
- **Firm followed by not-steep tends to be safe — low-to-medium confidence.** It started 4 of 5 and later stood at 7 of 10. Useful as a prior, not a rule.
- **Clear before the next row was a strong sign of no STORM — medium confidence.** I do not remember a clear predecessor producing storm. It still says nothing about sand.
- **Haze is only a risk signal, not a deterministic storm rule — high confidence in the uncertainty.** Haze+steep was safe 5 of 7 in the notebook. Haze+flat produced storm only 1 of 3. Firm+haze before the same slope sometimes produced safe rows and sometimes fatal storms.
- **Ground and dust behave like separate hazard channels.** Ground history predicts SAND; dust history predicts STORM. A row is safe only when neither channel is hazardous. Confidence: high.

The word “rules” overstates most relationships. Apart from soft+not-steep causing sand, the evidence looks probabilistic or dependent on information not represented by the card face and coarse slope.

## Sampling

Sampling was worth a turn when one channel was uncertain and the other could already be ruled out:

- Ground samples on rows 4, 8, and 13 resolved the remaining sand question.
- The run-4 row-4 ground sample directly found SAND.
- The run-4 row-11 dust sample directly found STORM and saved the run.
- The run-5 row-2 dust sample correctly established that row itself had no storm.

Sampling both channels on run-4 row 5 was expensive but justified because the previous SAND card gave me no ground rule and haze-to-tilted had mixed results. Both samples proved the row safe.

Sampling was not worth it when I already had a strong two-channel prediction, or when I sampled one row and then overgeneralized its result to the next. The first run’s ground sample was mechanically useful—it showed no current sand—but I misunderstood `soft` and still called danger. A non-hazard sample only resolves that channel on the current row; it does not make the next row safe.

The economy felt reasonable: a sample costs one turn, while an avoid costs two extra turns compared with going. Sampling one unresolved channel is usually the best bargain. Sampling both is only sustainable occasionally.

## How I used the map

I mainly trusted the explicit slope label: flat, tilted, or steep. I combined the next row’s label with the previous row’s ground and dust cards. That was essential for the reliable soft+not-steep sand rule.

I also watched:

- contour spacing and density;
- blue versus brown terrain;
- where the white route crossed into dense brown contours;
- tight bends and long steep segments;
- the rover’s position along the repeated route.

The visual terrain never gave me a rule I could defend. Row 11’s storm amid tight brown contours made the map feel meaningful, but row 3’s storm in the sparse blue area broke that theory. The route and slopes repeated across runs while card outcomes changed, so I eventually trusted the labeled slope more than the decorative-looking terrain.

## Feel: confusing, unfair, boring, fun

**Fun:** Deducing the soft+not-steep sand rule was satisfying. Using it to avoid row 12, finding row 11’s storm with one sample, and finishing run 4 with one turn left felt genuinely earned. The two independent hazard channels make selective sampling interesting.

**Confusing:** `sol 1`, `sol 2`, etc. changed every run, but the screen never explained what a “sol” was or which knowledge should transfer. The notebook’s tiny marks took several rows to parse. The rule rows ended with an `×` that looked like a failed test even when the text said “always 5 of 5”; I eventually assumed it was a delete control.

**Unfair:** Exact-looking repeats could disagree. In run 5, firm+haze followed by flat was safe and then stormy on consecutive rows. Firm+haze before steep was safe twice and later fatal. If the intended lesson is probability, “rule” and “always/sometimes” need stronger framing. If additional map detail decides the outcome, the screen did not teach me how to read it.

**Boring:** The opening route repeated enough that replaying the first few rows became mechanical. Re-rendering the same large map after every sample also made a two-sample decision feel slow.

## Information the screen failed to tell me

- What `sol` means and whether each run changes the underlying rules, only the deck, or both.
- Whether card+slope rules are deterministic, probabilistic, or missing another visible condition.
- A direct statement that safe means “neither SAND nor STORM.”
- A direct statement that ground and dust are separate hazard channels.
- Whether `firm`, `soft`, `clear`, and `haze` on a sample explicitly mean that channel is safe now. The legend implies this, but I misread it on my first visit.
- What the terminal `×` on each saved notebook rule does.
- Why the command says to keep one rule while the notebook visibly accumulated several.
- Whether contour color, contour spacing, route shape, or position have gameplay meaning beyond the printed slope.
- What happens when turns reach zero.

The scoring was learnable from results, but a compact explicit formula would help: completing a row appeared to give 1 point, a correct call another 1, and finishing another 3.
